The opportunity: EVs and hybrids in Pakistan

Why these cars are arriving now, which ones you will see, and what work they will bring.

You will learn

  • Why electric and hybrid cars are growing quickly in Pakistan
  • Which brands and models are on our roads
  • How an electric car is different to work on than a petrol car
  • Why independent workshops will be needed

1.1 What is changing

For many years the Pakistani car market was a small number of Japanese petrol models. That has changed. Since 2024, Chinese manufacturers have entered with battery electric cars and plug-in hybrids, and most of them now assemble cars locally or plan to.

Three things are pushing this change:

  • Fuel cost. Petrol is imported and expensive. Electricity per kilometre is much cheaper, especially for owners with solar panels at home.
  • Government policy. The New Energy Vehicle (NEV) Policy 2025–30 sets a target that 30% of new vehicles sold in 2030 should be electric, across bikes, rickshaws, cars, buses and trucks. It lowers duties on EV parts and supports local assembly.
  • Chinese technology and price. China makes most of the world's EV batteries. Chinese brands can offer a well-equipped EV or plug-in hybrid at a price close to a Japanese petrol SUV.

In Pakistan — the numbers (2026)

  • Target: 30% of new vehicle sales electric by 2030.
  • Target: 3,000 public charging stations by 2030. In early 2026 there were only around 30–50.
  • Local production of electric bikes and rickshaws rose from about 60,000 (2024–25) to about 210,000 (2025–26).
  • The PAVE scheme gives buyers a cash subsidy on electric bikes and rickshaws that use lithium batteries.
  • Regulator NEPRA set a special low electricity tariff for charging stations (base rate Rs 23.57 per unit, plus the station's margin).

Workshop tip

Do not forget the used Japanese hybrids. Tens of thousands of imported Toyota Aqua, Prius and Honda Vezel hybrids are already 8–15 years old. Their hybrid batteries are wearing out now. This is work that exists today, not in five years.

1.3 Why independent workshops matter

A new EV usually has a vehicle warranty of about 4–6 years and a battery warranty of around 8 years or 160,000 km. During warranty, owners go to the dealer.

After warranty, things change. Dealer labour rates and parts prices are high. A middle-class family with limited spare income will look for a trusted private workshop — the same way they do today for a Corolla or a Mehran. The problem is that very few private workshops understand high-voltage cars. Many mechanics are afraid of them, and they are right to be afraid if they are not trained.

Key idea

The cars are arriving faster than the technicians. A person who can safely diagnose and repair EVs and hybrids will have a skill that is in short supply for many years.

An EV still has suspension, steering, brakes, tyres, air-conditioning, lights and body work. Your normal mechanical skills remain useful. What is new is the high-voltage system and the amount of electronics and software.

1.5 What an EV technician actually does

  • Makes the car safe before any work (isolating the high-voltage system)
  • Reads fault codes and live data with a scan tool, and understands what they mean
  • Tests batteries, cables, motors and chargers with meters
  • Replaces parts: 12 V batteries, coolant pumps, chargers, contactors, sensors, drive units
  • Services cooling systems, brakes, air-conditioning and suspension
  • Explains problems honestly to the customer

At a higher level, after more training: opening battery packs, replacing modules, and repairing power electronics.

Summary

  • Fuel cost, government policy and Chinese technology are driving EVs and hybrids into Pakistan.
  • You will see Chinese BEVs and PHEVs, Japanese and Korean hybrids, and many electric bikes and rickshaws.
  • After warranty, owners need affordable private workshops, and trained technicians are scarce.
  • EVs have fewer mechanical parts but need electrical knowledge, diagnostic tools and strict safety.

Electricity basics

Volts, amps, ohms, watts and kilowatt-hours — the language of every EV specification.

You will learn

  • What voltage, current and resistance are, and how Ohm's law links them
  • The difference between power (kW) and energy (kWh)
  • The difference between AC and DC
  • What happens when cells are connected in series and in parallel

2.1 Voltage, current and resistance

Think of electricity as water in pipes.

  • Voltage (V) is the pressure pushing the electricity. Unit: volt (V).
  • Current (I) is the flow — how much electricity passes each second. Unit: ampere or amp (A).
  • Resistance (R) is anything that restricts the flow, like a narrow pipe. Unit: ohm (Ω).

More pressure gives more flow. More restriction gives less flow. This is Ohm's law:

V = I × R   I = V ÷ R   R = V ÷ I

2.2 Power: how fast energy is used

Power (P) is how quickly electrical energy is being used or delivered. Unit: watt (W). One kilowatt (kW) is 1,000 watts.

P = V × I

Worked example

An EV battery at 400 V supplies 250 A to the motor. What power is that?

P = V × I = 400 × 250 = 100,000 W = 100 kW

For comparison, one horsepower (hp) is about 0.75 kW. So 100 kW ≈ 134 hp.

2.3 Energy: how much work can be done

Energy is power used over time. In EVs we measure it in kilowatt-hours (kWh). It is the same "unit" that appears on your electricity bill.

Energy (kWh) = Power (kW) × Time (hours)

Key idea

kW is like the size of the tap. kWh is like the size of the water tank.
Motor power (kW) tells you how strongly the car can accelerate. Battery energy (kWh) tells you how far it can go. Many people confuse them. A technician must not.

Worked example

A car uses an average of 15 kW while cruising. Its battery holds 60 kWh. How long can it cruise?

Time = Energy ÷ Power = 60 ÷ 15 = 4 hours

Amp-hours (Ah)

Battery cells are often rated in amp-hours (Ah). A 100 Ah cell can give 100 A for 1 hour, or 10 A for 10 hours. To convert to energy, multiply by the voltage:

Energy (Wh) = Voltage (V) × Capacity (Ah)

Example: a 12 V, 50 Ah car battery stores 12 × 50 = 600 Wh = 0.6 kWh. An EV battery stores about 100 times more.

2.4 Why EVs use high voltage

Look again at P = V × I. To deliver 100 kW you can use high voltage and low current, or low voltage and high current.

VoltageCurrent needed for 100 kWCable needed
12 V8,333 AImpossible — thick as your arm
400 V250 AAbout as thick as your thumb
800 V125 AThinner and lighter again

Current causes heating in cables. The heat loss is Ploss = I² × R. If you halve the current, the heat loss falls to a quarter. That is why EVs use 400 V or 800 V: thinner cables, less wasted heat, faster charging. The price is danger — see Module 3.

2.5 DC and AC

  • Direct current (DC) flows in one direction only. Batteries store and give DC.
  • Alternating current (AC) changes direction many times each second. Mains electricity in Pakistan is AC: 230 V, 50 Hz (50 cycles per second). Factories and large chargers use three-phase AC at 400 V.

Key idea

The battery is DC. The mains is AC. The drive motor runs on three-phase AC. So every EV needs boxes that convert between them: a charger (AC → DC) and an inverter (DC → AC). You will meet them in Module 10.

2.6 Series and parallel

One battery cell gives only about 3 to 4 volts. To build a 400 V pack, cells are joined together. There are two ways.

Worked example

A pack has 120 cells in series. Each cell is 3.2 V and 150 Ah.

Pack voltage = 120 × 3.2 = 384 V
Pack energy = 384 V × 150 Ah = 57,600 Wh = 57.6 kWh

Picture it — a traffic light for your hands

Orange = stop and think. Treat every orange cable like a red traffic light: you do not pass until you have isolated, waited and proved dead. Red = positive, black = negative on every battery and every meter: "red to plus".

Workshop tip

Inside a 12 V wiring loom, wire colours are different on every make. Never guess a wire's job from its colour; use the wiring diagram, which lists the colour code and pin number of every wire. The three motor phase cables (U, V, W) are all orange, so they are identified by labels, not colours.

In this book's diagrams: orange lines are high voltage, red is 12 V positive or a meter's positive lead, black is negative, thin blue lines are low-voltage signal and control wires, and grey is mechanical drive.

Summary

  • Voltage pushes, current flows, resistance restricts. V = I × R.
  • Power P = V × I in kW. Energy = power × time in kWh.
  • kW = how strong. kWh = how far.
  • High voltage lets EVs carry big power with small current.
  • Batteries are DC. Mains and motors are AC.
  • Series adds voltage. Parallel adds capacity.
  • Orange = high voltage. Red = positive. Black = negative.

High-voltage safety

The rules that keep you alive. Learn this module until you can explain it to someone else.

You will learn

  • Why EV voltage is deadly and how electricity injures the body
  • How the car protects you, and where that protection ends
  • The personal protective equipment (PPE) you must use
  • The safe isolation procedure, step by step
  • What to do in an emergency

3.1 How much voltage is dangerous?

International vehicle standards define high voltage as anything above 60 V DC or 30 V AC. Below this, dry skin normally protects you. Above this, current can pass through your body.

3.2 What electricity does to the body

It is the current through your body that injures you. The voltage decides how much current flows. Your body's resistance is roughly 1,000 Ω when skin is damp or broken.

Worked example

You touch 400 V across your body with sweaty hands (R ≈ 1,000 Ω).

I = V ÷ R = 400 ÷ 1,000 = 0.4 A = 400 mA. The table below shows this is many times a deadly current.

Table 3.1 — Effect of current through the body
CurrentEffect
1 mASlight tingle
10–20 mAMuscles lock. You cannot let go.
50–100 mAHeart rhythm is disturbed (fibrillation). Death is likely without fast help.
Over 200 mASevere burns, heart stops, damage to internal organs

There are three ways high voltage hurts you:

  1. Electric shock — current through the body, especially hand to hand across the chest.
  2. Arc flash — if a metal tool bridges two battery terminals, thousands of amps flow. The metal explodes into a flash hotter than a welding arc. It burns skin and eyes in a fraction of a second.
  3. Fire and chemical — a damaged lithium cell can overheat, vent toxic gas and burn (thermal runaway, Module 8).

In Pakistan

Summer heat means sweat. Sweaty skin has low resistance, so the same voltage pushes more current through you. Monsoon rain and flooding add water to the mix. Our conditions make high-voltage discipline more important, not less.

3.3 How the car protects you

Manufacturers build several layers of protection into every EV and hybrid.

ProtectionWhat it does
Orange cablesAll high-voltage cables and connectors are coloured orange. Orange means: do not touch unless the system is proven dead.
Isolated systemThe high-voltage circuit is not connected to the car body. Both + and − are insulated from the chassis ("floating"). The 12 V system, in contrast, uses the body as its negative.
ContactorsLarge relays inside the battery pack. When the car is off, they open and disconnect the battery from everything outside the pack.
HVIL (high-voltage interlock loop)A small low-voltage wire loop running through every HV connector and cover. If a connector is unplugged, the loop breaks and the contactors open.
Insulation monitoringThe car constantly measures the resistance between the HV system and the body. If it falls too low, it warns the driver or refuses to start.
Service disconnectA plug or switch that a technician removes to break the HV circuit, often in the middle of the pack.
Crash cut-offWhen airbags fire, the contactors are opened at once, sometimes by a small explosive fuse (pyro-fuse).
Discharge circuitCapacitors in the inverter store charge. After switch-off, the car drains them, usually within seconds to a few minutes.

Danger — where the protection ends

  • Inside the battery pack is always live. Contactors and the service plug only disconnect the pack from the rest of the car. The cells themselves cannot be switched off.
  • Protection systems can fail, especially after a crash, flood or fire, or after a bad repair.
  • Capacitors can hold charge if the discharge circuit has failed.
  • So we never trust the car. We always measure.

Workshop tip

Ordinary rubber or mechanic's gloves are not insulating gloves. Real Class 0 gloves have the class and voltage printed on the cuff. They cost more, they are worth more, and they must be re-tested or replaced regularly (typically every 6 months).

3.5 The workshop area

  • A clean, dry bay. No water on the floor.
  • Barrier or cones around the car, and a sign: "DANGER — HIGH VOLTAGE — DO NOT TOUCH".
  • A second person nearby who knows what you are doing and how to help.
  • An insulated rescue hook (a non-conducting pole) to pull a person away from a live part.
  • Fire extinguisher (CO₂ or dry powder for small electrical fires) and access to plenty of water.
  • Insulating rubber mat to stand on.

3.6 The safe isolation procedure

Every manufacturer has its own exact procedure. You must follow the service manual for the car in front of you. But all procedures follow the same logic: switch off → disconnect → secure → wait → prove dead.

  1. Prepare the car. Park on a level, dry surface. Select P, apply parking brake, chock the wheels. Unplug any charging cable.
  2. Switch off and remove the key. Take the smart key at least 5 metres away so the car cannot wake up. Check the "READY" light is off.
  3. Put on PPE and set up the area. Gloves checked, glasses on, signs and barrier in place.
  4. Disconnect the 12 V battery negative terminal. Without 12 V, the contactors cannot close.
  5. Remove the service disconnect (wearing gloves). Keep it in your pocket or a locked box so nobody can refit it. This is "lock out, tag out".
  6. Wait. The manual gives a time, usually 5 to 10 minutes, for capacitors to discharge.
  7. Prove dead — the three-step test. (a) Test your meter on a known live source, such as the 12 V battery. (b) Measure at the HV point you will work on: + to −, + to body, − to body. All must read near 0 V. (c) Test your meter on the known source again to prove it still works.
  8. Only now begin work. If you leave the car and return, prove dead again.

Key idea — "prove, test, prove"

A meter with a broken lead or a flat battery reads 0 V on a live circuit. That is why you check the meter before and after the dead test. Many electricians have died trusting a faulty meter.

3.7 Special danger situations

  • Accident-damaged cars. Insulation may be cut and the body may be live. Treat every orange cable and the pack case as live. Look for leaking fluid, smoke, a sweet chemical smell, or hissing sounds.
  • Flood-damaged cars. Water inside a pack causes corrosion and short circuits that can start a fire days or weeks later. Store such cars outdoors, away from buildings and other cars.
  • Charging. Never work on a car while it is plugged in.
  • Towing. Many EVs must be moved on a flatbed truck. Turning the driven wheels spins the motor, which generates voltage.
  • Hybrids. The engine can start by itself when the car is in READY mode. Always switch fully off.

3.8 Emergencies

If someone is receiving a shock

  1. Do not touch them. You will become the second victim.
  2. Switch off the source if you can do it instantly.
  3. Pull them clear with the insulated rescue hook, or dry wood or thick dry cloth — never metal, never wet.
  4. Call for help: Rescue 1122.
  5. If they are not breathing, start CPR and continue until help arrives. Every EV workshop should have at least one person trained in first aid.
  6. Anyone who has had an electric shock must see a doctor, even if they feel fine. Heart problems can appear hours later.

If a battery is smoking or burning

  1. Get everyone out and away. The smoke is toxic.
  2. Call the fire service (Rescue 1122 / 16) and say clearly: "electric vehicle battery fire".
  3. If safe, move other cars away. Do not try to be a hero.
  4. A lithium battery fire needs a very large amount of water for a long time to cool it. It can re-ignite hours later.

Summary

  • Above 60 V DC is high voltage. EVs use 200–800 V DC. This kills.
  • Orange = high voltage. Never touch unless proven dead.
  • The car has many protections, but the inside of the battery is always live and protections can fail.
  • Class 0 gloves, eye protection, insulated tools, CAT III 1,000 V meter, no metal jewellery.
  • Switch off → disconnect 12 V → remove service plug → wait → prove dead (prove, test, prove).
  • Never work alone. Never touch a shock victim with bare hands.
