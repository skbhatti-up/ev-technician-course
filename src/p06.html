17.2 The multimeter

Rules that prevent accidents

  • Check the CAT rating. For vehicle high voltage use CAT III 1,000 V (or CAT IV 600 V). Cheap unrated meters can explode in your hand on a high-energy circuit.
  • Leads in the right sockets. If the red lead is left in the amps (A) socket and you touch it across a voltage, the meter is a dead short. On an HV battery this causes an arc flash. Many technicians tape over the amps sockets on their HV meter and use a clamp for current.
  • Select the function before you connect. DC volts for batteries; AC volts for mains.
  • Never measure resistance on a live circuit.
  • Inspect the leads every time. Use leads with shrouded plugs and finger guards.
  • One hand where possible. Clip one lead on, then probe with one hand, so current cannot pass hand to hand across your chest.

17.3 The insulation tester

A multimeter measures resistance using a few volts. Damaged insulation can look perfect at 3 V and break down at 400 V. An insulation tester (megohmmeter, often called a "megger") applies 500 V or 1,000 V DC at a tiny, safe current and measures resistance in megohms.

  • Used only on components that are isolated, disconnected and proven dead, following the manual. Sensitive electronics may need to be unplugged first.
  • Healthy parts normally read tens or hundreds of megohms. The service manual gives the minimum acceptable value.
  • The tester's output is itself high voltage. Keep hands clear and let it discharge the part before removing the leads.
  • Never use an insulation tester across battery cells or on live circuits.

Oscilloscope and CAN tools

  • A multimeter shows one number. An oscilloscope draws voltage against time, so you can see signals that change thousands of times a second: resolver outputs, CAN messages, the control pilot's square wave, sensor signals.
  • A CAN interface (training centres often use one called PCAN) connects a laptop to the bus and lists every message with its label and data. Decoding messages is advanced work, but simply seeing whether a unit is transmitting at all is a powerful test.

Summary

  • Start with a CAT III 1,000 V meter, a capable scan tool and a 12 V battery tester.
  • Voltage: across. Current: clamp around one wire. Resistance: power off.
  • Wrong lead socket + high voltage = arc flash.
  • An insulation tester finds faults a multimeter cannot see.
  • Live data — especially cell voltage spread and insulation resistance — is the heart of EV diagnosis.

Fault finding

A method for diagnosis, how to read fault codes, and six real-world cases.

You will learn

  • A step-by-step diagnostic method
  • How fault codes are structured
  • How to approach the most common EV and hybrid complaints
  • When to stop and refer the job

18.1 The method

Key idea

A fault code is a clue, not a verdict. The code tells you which circuit or value the computer is unhappy with. It does not tell you which part to replace. "Battery over-temperature" might be the battery — or a blocked fan, a failed pump, low refrigerant, or a faulty sensor. Your job is to prove which.

18.2 Reading fault codes

Standard diagnostic trouble codes (DTCs) have a letter and four characters.

First letterSystemExamples
PPowertrain (includes HV battery, motor, inverter, charging)P0A80 — replace hybrid battery pack
P0AA6 — HV system insulation fault
P0A0A — HV interlock (HVIL) circuit
P0A94 — DC-DC converter performance
P0AA1 — battery positive contactor stuck closed
BBodyAirbags, locks, climate
CChassisABS, steering, brake system
UNetworkU0100 — lost communication with a control unit

Codes beginning P0 are standard across makers; many hybrid and EV faults sit in the P0A–P0E range. Codes beginning P1, and many others, are specific to the maker; you need that maker's information to interpret them.

18.3 Six common cases

Case 1 — "The car is completely dead / will not go to READY"

  1. Measure the 12 V battery. Below about 12.0 V → charge or replace, then retest.
  2. Check main 12 V fuses and battery terminals.
  3. Brake pedal switch (the car needs to see the pedal pressed), key detection, gear in P.
  4. Scan for codes: HVIL, pre-charge failure, contactor, insulation fault, BMS.
  5. Has anyone worked on the car recently? A connector not fully seated opens the HVIL.

Case 2 — "Insulation fault" / "leakage" warning

  1. Ask when it appears: in rain? after washing? when the A/C or heater is switched on? only when charging?
  2. Read the live insulation resistance value. Watch it while switching HV consumers on and off (A/C, heater).
  3. With the system safely isolated, disconnect HV components one at a time and insulation-test each: A/C compressor, PTC heater, on-board charger, motor, cables.
  4. The reading that is low identifies the faulty part or cable.
  5. If everything outside the pack tests good, the fault is inside the battery — a specialist job.

Frequent causes: wrong oil in the A/C compressor, water in a connector or the charge port, coolant leaking into a component, rodent-damaged cables.

Case 3 — "Range has dropped"

  1. Ask about recent changes: season, route, speed, new tyres, roof rack.
  2. Tyre pressures, brake drag (spin each wheel; feel for hot wheels after a drive), alignment.
  3. Scan: SOH, cell voltage spread, battery temperatures.
  4. If SOH is good and the cells are balanced, the battery is healthy. Explain consumption and conditions.

Case 4 — "It will not charge"

Follow the table in Module 14.6. The quickest test is substitution: try a different charger and cable. If the car charges elsewhere, the fault is in the customer's equipment or house supply, not the car.

Case 5 — "Low power / tortoise light, mostly in summer"

  1. Scan for temperature-related codes and look at live temperatures for battery, motor and inverter.
  2. Coolant levels in each reservoir. Pumps running? Radiator and condenser clean? Fans working?
  3. A/C performance (because the battery depends on it).
  4. Hybrids: battery fan and ducts.

Case 6 — Fault appears straight after another repair

Go back to what was touched. An unplugged or half-latched connector, a pinched wire, an earth strap left off, air left in a coolant loop, or a part that needed coding. The most recent work is the most likely cause.

18.4 Know your limits

Stop and refer the job when…

  • the fault is inside the battery pack and you are not trained and equipped for pack work;
  • the car has crash or flood damage to HV components;
  • you see signs of overheating, swelling, leaking or smell from the battery;
  • you do not have the service information for that model;
  • the repair needs a tool or software you do not have.

Saying "this is beyond what I can do safely; here is who can help" is professional. Guessing with 400 volts is not.

Summary

  • Listen, confirm, basics, scan, research, test, repair, verify.
  • Codes are clues. Prove the cause by measurement.
  • P = powertrain, B = body, C = chassis, U = network.
  • Always start with the 12 V battery and a visual check.
  • For insulation faults, isolate and test one component at a time.
  • Know when to stop.

19.2 The battery health check

This is a service customers value and will pay for. With a capable scan tool:

  1. Record SOH and odometer.
  2. Record highest and lowest cell voltage at rest, and the difference.
  3. Record highest and lowest battery temperature.
  4. Record the insulation resistance.
  5. Note any stored battery or charging fault codes.
  6. If possible, repeat the cell voltage reading during a short acceleration: a weak cell drops much more than the others.
  7. Give the customer a simple written report, and keep a copy. Next year you can show the trend.

19.4 Storage and customer advice

  • Leaving a car for weeks: store at 40–60% charge, in shade. Do not leave it plugged into a poor-quality supply.
  • The 12 V battery can go flat during long storage. Advise a periodic wake-up drive or a 12 V maintainer if the maker allows.
  • Keep records of every job: date, kilometres, readings, parts. Good records build customer trust and protect you if there is a dispute.

Summary

  • EV service is inspection-heavy: scan, battery report, 12 V, tyres, brakes, coolant, under-body.
  • Brake fluid every 2 years; coolant and gear oil as specified.
  • Hybrids need all petrol-engine servicing as well.
  • A written battery health report is valuable to owners and buyers.

Electric bikes and rickshaws

The biggest EV market in Pakistan by numbers, and the easiest place to start earning.

You will learn

  • The parts of an electric two- or three-wheeler
  • The battery options and how to compare them
  • How to diagnose the most common faults
  • The safety points specific to small EVs

20.1 Why this market matters

More than 200,000 electric bikes and rickshaws were produced in Pakistan in 2025–26, over three times the year before. Government subsidies under the PAVE scheme support models with lithium batteries. These vehicles are simple, the tools are cheap, and the customers are everywhere. For a new technician, this is the fastest route to real experience and income, and every principle is the same as in a car — just smaller.

20.2 The system

PartNotes
Battery48, 60 or 72 V. Capacity 20–50 Ah on bikes; more on rickshaws (5–9 kWh).
ControllerA small inverter. Rated by voltage and maximum current (for example 72 V, 50 A). Has a low-voltage cut-off to protect the battery.
Hub motorBLDC, 1–3 kW, built into the wheel. Three thick phase wires and five thin Hall-sensor wires (5 V, earth, three signals).
ThrottleA Hall sensor giving roughly 0.8 V (closed) to 4.2 V (full) on a 5 V supply.
Brake switchesTell the controller to cut power when a brake lever is pulled.
DC-DC converterSteps battery voltage down to 12 V for lights and horn.
ChargerExternal. Must match the battery's voltage and chemistry.
BMSInside every lithium pack. Lead-acid packs have none.

Danger — "small" does not mean safe

  • A "72 V" pack reaches 84–88 V when fully charged or charging. That is above the 60 V high-voltage limit. A "60 V" pack reaches about 70 V.
  • These batteries can deliver hundreds of amps into a short circuit. A spanner across the terminals becomes red-hot instantly. Burns and fires are far more common on bikes than shocks.
  • Remove rings and watches. Disconnect the battery (or switch off its breaker) before working. Insulate the terminals.

"Graphene battery" in this market means a lead-acid battery with a carbon additive in the plates. It is a little better than plain lead-acid, but it is still lead-acid.

Worked example — range

Battery 72 V, 32 Ah → 72 × 32 = 2,304 Wh. Consumption about 35 Wh/km.

Range = 2,304 ÷ 35 ≈ 66 km when new (lead-acid gives less in practice because it should not be fully discharged).

In Pakistan

Water is the great enemy of cheap e-bikes. Riding through monsoon water floods hub motors, controllers and connectors. Opening, drying, cleaning and re-sealing a hub motor, and replacing rusted bearings and Hall sensors, is steady work every rainy season.

20.5 Lead-acid to lithium conversions

Many owners will ask you to replace worn lead-acid blocks with a lithium pack. It can be a good upgrade if done properly:

  • Choose a pack with a real BMS, from a supplier who states the chemistry, cell count and maximum current.
  • The BMS continuous current rating must exceed the controller's maximum current.
  • Replace the charger. A lead-acid charger must never be used on a lithium pack. Use the charger designed for that pack's chemistry and cell count.
  • Check the controller's low-voltage cut-off suits the new pack.
  • Mount the pack firmly, protected from water and impact, with a fuse close to the battery.

Danger — charging fires

Most e-bike battery fires happen while charging, usually indoors at night, with a cheap pack, a wrong charger, or a damaged battery. Advise every customer: use only the correct charger; charge in an open, ventilated place away from the exit and from anything that burns; do not charge a swollen, dropped or water-damaged battery; do not leave it charging all night unattended.

Summary

  • Battery → controller → BLDC hub motor, with throttle and brake switches as inputs.
  • "72 V" really means up to 88 V and very high short-circuit current.
  • Lead-acid ("graphene") is cheap and short-lived; lithium is lighter and lasts longer but must be good quality.
  • Test lead-acid blocks individually under load.
  • Match the charger to the battery. Most fires happen during charging.

21.5 Habits of a professional

  1. Safety first, every time. No shortcut is worth your life. Stop anyone who is working unsafely, including your seniors.
  2. Measure, don't guess. Diagnose before you replace.
  3. Be honest. Tell the customer what you found, what you are sure of, and what you are not. Never invent a fault. Reputation is your real capital.
  4. Keep records. Readings, photos, parts fitted.
  5. Keep it clean. A tidy bay, clean tools, covered seats. HV connectors must be spotless and dry.
  6. Keep learning. This technology changes every year. Read service information, follow training, ask questions.
  7. Know your limits — and say so.

In ten years, electric and hybrid vehicles will be ordinary in Pakistan, and the people who learned early — properly and safely — will be the ones training others and running the workshops. Study this book until you own it. Then go and get your hands dirty, with your gloves on.

How to make it stick

  • Draw it. After each module, close the book and redraw its main diagram from memory. Check, correct, repeat the next day.
  • Teach it. Explain one "picture" to a friend or family member who knows nothing about cars. If they understand, you do.
  • Find it. Stand next to any EV or hybrid and name what you can see without touching: orange cables, charge port, coolant reservoirs, the compressor, the 12 V battery.
  • Come back to it. Repeat each quiz after one day, one week and one month. Memory needs spaced repeats, not one long sitting.
