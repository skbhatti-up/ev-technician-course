Electric motors

How a motor turns electricity into motion, and the types used in cars, bikes and rickshaws.

You will learn

  • The basic principle of every electric motor
  • The parts of a traction motor
  • The main motor types and where each is used
  • The shape of the motor's torque–speed curve
  • Common motor faults

9.1 The principle

You know that magnets push and pull each other: like poles repel, unlike poles attract. You also know from school that a coil of wire carrying current becomes a magnet (an electromagnet), and that you can reverse its poles by reversing the current.

A motor uses these two facts. The outer part, the stator, has coils of copper wire. The inner part, the rotor, has magnets. By switching current through the stator coils in the right order, we create a magnetic field that rotates around the stator. The rotor magnets are dragged round with it. That rotation is what turns the wheels.

Picture it — the donkey and the carrot

Hold a carrot on a stick in front of a donkey and walk in a circle: the donkey follows the carrot round and round. The stator's rotating magnetic field is the carrot. The rotor magnets are the donkey. Move the carrot faster (higher frequency) and the donkey turns faster. That is all a motor is.

Three-phase AC (Module 2) is perfect for this: three sets of coils, fed by three currents one-third of a cycle apart, naturally produce a smoothly rotating field.

Danger — a spinning motor is a generator

A permanent-magnet motor produces voltage at its terminals whenever the rotor turns, even with the battery disconnected. If the driven wheels are turned — by towing, pushing fast, or spinning a wheel on a lift — the orange cables can become live. Never turn the wheels of an EV with HV parts exposed.

9.4 The torque–speed curve

Torque is turning force, measured in newton-metres (Nm). This graph is the single most important picture for understanding how an EV drives.

  • From 0 rpm up to the base speed, the motor can give its maximum torque. The limit here is the maximum current the inverter and battery can supply. Power rises steadily with speed.
  • Above base speed, the motor has reached its maximum power. The limit here is voltage. Power stays roughly level, so torque must fall as speed rises.

This is why an EV feels so quick away from traffic lights, and why it needs only one gear.

9.5 Motor ratings on a specification sheet

  • Peak power (kW) — the maximum, available for a short time (typically 30 seconds). This is the advertised figure.
  • Continuous (rated) power (kW) — what it can give all day without overheating. Often about half the peak.
  • Peak torque (Nm) — maximum turning force.
  • Maximum speed (rpm) — typically 14,000–20,000.

Summary

  • A rotating magnetic field in the stator pulls the rotor round.
  • PMSM is the standard car motor. Induction motors have no magnets. BLDC hub motors drive bikes.
  • Full torque from zero speed up to base speed; constant power above it.

  • A turning permanent-magnet motor generates voltage — never spin the wheels with HV parts exposed.

Power electronics

The boxes that convert and control electrical power: inverter, DC-DC converter, on-board charger and PDU.

You will learn

  • How an inverter makes three-phase AC from battery DC
  • How the inverter controls motor speed and torque
  • What the DC-DC converter, on-board charger and PDU do
  • Common faults and simple checks

10.1 The inverter

The inverter (also called motor control unit, MCU, or power control unit, PCU) sits between the battery and the motor. Inside are six fast electronic switches arranged in three pairs — one pair for each motor phase.

How switching makes AC

The switches can only be fully on or fully off. To make a smooth AC wave, the inverter turns them on and off very quickly (around 5,000–20,000 times per second) and varies how long each pulse lasts. Wide pulses give a high average voltage; narrow pulses give a low one. This is pulse-width modulation (PWM). The motor windings smooth the pulses into a near-perfect sine-wave current.

Picture it — the tap you can only open fully

Imagine a tap that can only be fully open or fully shut, filling a bucket with a small hole in it. Open it for long bursts and the water level rises; open it for short bursts and the level falls. By changing only the length of the bursts, you can hold the water at any level you like. The inverter's switches are that tap, and the level in the bucket is the voltage the motor feels.

Key idea — how the driver's foot controls the motor

The inverter controls two things:
• The frequency of the AC sets the motor speed.
• The current (amplitude) sets the motor torque.
Press the accelerator → the vehicle computer asks for more torque → the inverter sends more current. To reverse, it simply swaps the order of the phases.

Inside the inverter

  • Power module — the six switches. Older and cheaper designs use IGBTs (silicon). Newer ones use SiC MOSFETs (silicon carbide), which waste less energy as heat and are essential for 800 V systems.
  • DC-link capacitor — a large capacitor across the DC input that smooths the supply. It stores a dangerous charge.
  • Gate driver and control board — the electronics that decide when to fire each switch.
  • Current sensors — on the phase outputs.
  • Cooling plate — coolant flows under the power module.

Danger

The DC-link capacitor holds battery voltage after switch-off until the discharge circuit drains it. This is the reason for the waiting time in the isolation procedure, and the reason you measure before touching.

10.2 The DC-DC converter

A petrol car has an alternator driven by the engine to charge the 12 V battery. An EV has no alternator. Instead a DC-DC converter takes high voltage from the main battery and steps it down to about 13.5–14.8 V. Typical output is 1.5–3 kW (over 100 A).

Workshop tip — a simple, safe test

Measure the 12 V battery voltage with the car off (expect about 12.4–12.8 V) and again with the car in READY (expect about 13.5–14.8 V). If the voltage does not rise in READY, the DC-DC converter is not charging. This is a low-voltage test you can do without touching any orange cable.

10.3 The on-board charger (OBC)

The battery needs DC. The mains supplies AC. The on-board charger converts one to the other when you plug into an AC socket or wallbox. Its power rating sets the maximum AC charging speed:

During DC fast charging the big converter is inside the charging station, and current goes straight to the battery. The on-board charger is bypassed (Module 14).

Many Chinese EVs have a bidirectional charger that can also work backwards, giving 230 V AC from the car battery through an adapter. This is called V2L (vehicle-to-load).

In Pakistan

V2L is a real selling point here: during load-shedding an EV can run fans, lights, a fridge or tools for many hours. Customers will ask you about it. They may also damage it by overloading it or using poor-quality adapters, which gives you repair work.

10.4 The power distribution unit (PDU)

The PDU is the HV junction box. From the battery, power is shared out — through fuses and sometimes relays — to the inverter, on-board charger, DC-DC converter, A/C compressor and heater. When one HV consumer stops working (for example the A/C compressor), a blown HV fuse in the PDU is a possible cause. HV fuses are only checked or changed with the system isolated and proven dead.

10.5 The boost converter in Toyota hybrids

Toyota hybrid batteries are only about 150–250 V. To make the motors more powerful and efficient, a boost converter inside the inverter assembly raises this to as much as 600–650 V for the motors. So the voltage inside a Toyota inverter is far higher than the battery voltage written on the label.

10.6 Cooling and common faults

Power electronics are about 95–98% efficient, but 2–5% of 150 kW is still several kilowatts of heat. They share a coolant loop with an electric pump and a radiator.

Workshop tip

On older Toyota hybrids, a failed inverter coolant pump is a classic fault. It is cheap. If ignored, it destroys the inverter, which is not cheap. Checking that the coolant in the inverter reservoir is moving (with the car in READY) takes ten seconds.

Summary

  • The inverter uses six switches and PWM to turn DC into three-phase AC.
  • Frequency sets speed; current sets torque.
  • The DC-DC converter charges the 12 V system: expect 13.5–14.8 V in READY.
  • The on-board charger converts mains AC to DC and sets the AC charging speed.
  • The PDU distributes and fuses the high voltage.
  • Power electronics need liquid cooling. Check coolant and pumps first.

Performance: how battery and motor shape the car

Acceleration, top speed, range and running cost — and the numbers behind them.

You will learn

  • How torque, power and speed are related
  • How motor power and battery power together limit acceleration
  • How battery energy and consumption decide range
  • Why real range differs from the brochure, and how to explain it to customers

11.1 Torque, speed and power

Power is torque multiplied by rotational speed. In workshop units:

Power (kW) = Torque (Nm) × Speed (rpm) ÷ 9,550

Worked example

A motor makes 310 Nm at 4,600 rpm. Power = 310 × 4,600 ÷ 9,550 = 149 kW.

The same motor at 12,000 rpm can still make about 150 kW, so its torque there is 150 × 9,550 ÷ 12,000 = 119 Nm. Same power, much less torque — the constant-power region from Figure 9.2.

  • Torque (multiplied by the gear ratio) decides how hard the car pushes off from rest and at low speed.
  • Power decides how well it keeps accelerating at higher speed, how it climbs long hills, and its top speed.

11.2 From motor torque to acceleration

Worked example — why EVs launch so hard

Motor torque 310 Nm. Reduction gear ratio 10:1. Wheel radius 0.33 m. Car mass 1,750 kg.

1. Torque at the wheels = 310 × 10 = 3,100 Nm (ignoring small losses)
2. Driving force = torque ÷ wheel radius = 3,100 ÷ 0.33 ≈ 9,400 N
3. Acceleration = force ÷ mass = 9,400 ÷ 1,750 ≈ 5.4 m/s²

That is more than half of gravity, available instantly from 0 km/h. A typical family petrol car manages about half of this in first gear, and only once the engine has revved up.

11.3 The weakest link sets the limit

How the battery limits power

The motor may be rated at 150 kW, but it can only deliver what the battery can supply.

Worked example

A 60 kWh pack at 400 V can safely give 400 A. Maximum battery power = 400 × 400 = 160 kW. The 150 kW motor can be fully used.

Now the same motor with a small 20 kWh pack of the same cells: one-third of the cells in parallel, so about 133 A. Maximum = 400 × 133 = 53 kW. The motor is still a 150 kW motor, but the car performs like a 53 kW car.

This explains several things you will see:

  • Long-range versions are often faster. A bigger battery can supply more current, so the maker fits or unlocks a more powerful motor.
  • Hybrids need "power cells". An HEV battery of 1.5 kWh giving 40 kW is working at about 27C. These cells are designed for high power, not high energy.
  • PHEVs feel strongest with charge in the battery. With the battery depleted, less electrical power is available to help the engine.
  • Power is reduced when the battery is cold, very hot, nearly empty or aged. Internal resistance is higher, so the BMS lowers the current limit. The customer complains of "low power"; the scan tool shows the BMS limit as the reason.

Key idea

Motor kW is what the car could do. Battery kW (which depends on battery size, chemistry, temperature, charge level and health) is what it is allowed to do right now. Performance is whichever is lower.

11.5 Range

Range (km) = Usable battery energy (Wh) ÷ Consumption (Wh per km)

Consumption is the EV's "fuel average". A small city car may use 110–130 Wh/km; a compact SUV 150–180 Wh/km; a large SUV or pickup 200–280 Wh/km. An electric motorcycle uses only 25–40 Wh/km.

Worked example

Usable battery 60 kWh = 60,000 Wh. Consumption 160 Wh/km.

Range = 60,000 ÷ 160 = 375 km

On the motorway at 120 km/h with A/C, consumption rises to 230 Wh/km: range = 60,000 ÷ 230 = 261 km.

Brochure range and real range

Range is measured on standard laboratory tests. Different tests give different answers for the same car:

  • NEDC and CLTC — older and Chinese tests. Gentle and optimistic. Often quoted in Pakistani advertising.
  • WLTP — the current European test. More realistic.
  • Real mixed driving in Pakistan is usually around 70–80% of the NEDC or CLTC figure, and less on the motorway in summer.

Workshop tip

"My range has dropped" is one of the most common EV complaints. Before blaming the battery, check: tyre pressures, brake drag, wheel alignment, driving speed, A/C use, and the season. Then read the SOH on the scan tool. Most of the time the battery is fine and the cause is one of the simple things.

11.6 Efficiency and running cost

From battery to wheels, an EV is about 85–90% efficient. A petrol engine turns only about 25–35% of the fuel's energy into motion; the rest is heat. This is the basic reason an EV costs less per kilometre.

Worked example — cost per 100 km (put in today's prices)

EV: 16 kWh per 100 km × Rs 50 per unit = Rs 800
Petrol car: 12 km per litre → 8.3 litres per 100 km × Rs 270 per litre = Rs 2,240

With home solar, the EV figure can be far lower. Prices change, so always redo this sum with current rates.

Summary

  • Power (kW) = torque (Nm) × rpm ÷ 9,550.
  • Torque × gear ratio gives launch; power gives high-speed performance.
  • Performance is limited by the weakest of: battery power, inverter current, motor, tyres.
  • Battery power depends on size, chemistry, temperature, SOC and health.
  • Range = usable energy ÷ consumption. Speed, A/C, tyres and driving style change consumption.
  • Expect about 70–80% of NEDC/CLTC range in real use.

Transmission, regenerative braking and chassis

The mechanical side of an EV: one gear, two kinds of braking, and a heavy car on four tyres.

You will learn

  • Why EVs have a single-speed reduction gear
  • How regenerative braking works and when it is limited
  • How regenerative and friction brakes are blended
  • Brake, tyre and lifting points specific to EVs

12.1 The reduction gear

A petrol engine works well only in a narrow speed band, so it needs 5 or more gears. An electric motor gives useful torque from 0 to over 16,000 rpm, so one fixed gear ratio is enough. A typical ratio is between 8:1 and 12:1: the motor turns about ten times for each turn of the wheels.

  • Reverse is done by spinning the motor backwards. There is no reverse gear.
  • Park uses a locking pawl in the gear unit, usually moved by a small electric actuator, plus the electric parking brake.
  • Neutral simply means the inverter sends no current.
  • The unit has its own gear oil (the specific fluid named by the maker). Some are "filled for life"; many specify a change at set intervals. Follow the service schedule.

Hybrids are different: they have special transmissions that mix engine and motor power. See Module 13.

12.2 Regenerative braking

When the driver lifts off the accelerator or presses the brake, the inverter makes the motor work as a generator. The wheels drive the motor; the motor resists; the car slows; and electricity flows back into the battery. Energy that a normal car throws away as brake heat is recovered.

Picture it — the bicycle dynamo

Switch on a bicycle's dynamo lamp and pedalling becomes harder. The dynamo is taking energy from your legs and turning it into electricity. Regenerative braking is the same thing on purpose: the motor becomes the dynamo, the "harder to pedal" feeling is what slows the car, and the electricity goes back into the battery instead of a lamp.

  • In city driving, regeneration can recover roughly 15–25% of the energy used.
  • Most cars let the driver choose the strength (low / standard / high). Strong regeneration allows one-pedal driving.
  • Brake lights come on automatically when regeneration is slowing the car firmly.

When regeneration is reduced or switched off

SituationReason
Battery at or near 100%There is nowhere to put the energy

Workshop tip

A customer who charges to 100% at the top of a hill station and then drives down will find the car "does not slow like before". That is normal: a full battery cannot take regeneration, so the friction brakes do all the work. Explain it; do not go looking for a fault.

12.3 Brake blending

Every EV and hybrid still has normal hydraulic friction brakes with discs and pads. The car's brake computer decides, moment by moment, how much braking comes from the motor and how much from the pads. The driver should feel one smooth pedal.

To make blending possible, and because there is no engine vacuum for a normal brake servo, EVs use an electric brake booster or a full brake-by-wire unit. The pedal is connected to a sensor and a "pedal feel simulator"; an electric motor or pump creates the hydraulic pressure.

12.4 Brake service on EVs

Because the motor does most of the braking, pads and discs last a very long time. The problem is the opposite of wear: lack of use.

  • Discs rust, especially at the rear, and after the monsoon.
  • Caliper slide pins seize; pads stick in their carriers; brakes drag and waste range.
  • Service: at least once a year, remove the pads, clean and lubricate the slide pins and pad contact points, and check the discs.
  • Brake fluid still absorbs moisture. Change it every 2 years or as specified.
  • Electric parking brakes need the scan tool's service mode to wind back the rear calipers before changing pads.
  • Bleeding a brake-by-wire system often needs a scan-tool procedure.

12.5 Tyres, suspension and lifting

  • EVs are 300–500 kg heavier than similar petrol cars and have instant torque. Tyres wear faster. Fit tyres with the correct load index; many are marked for EV use (lower rolling resistance, lower noise).
  • Rotate tyres regularly and check alignment. Poor alignment costs range as well as rubber.
  • Suspension bushes, ball joints and dampers carry more weight; on rough roads they wear sooner.

Danger — lifting and jacking

  • Never place a jack or lift pad under the battery pack. You can crush cells and cause an internal short. Use only the lifting points shown in the manual, usually on the sills.
  • Watch for speed breakers and stones: check the battery under-tray for dents and scrapes at every service.
  • Removing a battery pack (300–600 kg) needs a proper battery lifting table. It also changes the car's balance on the lift.
  • Most EVs must be transported on a flatbed, not towed with driven wheels on the ground.

In Pakistan

High speed breakers, broken roads and flooded underpasses are a real threat to under-floor batteries. An under-body inspection — looking for dents, torn covers, damaged coolant pipes and water marks — is a valuable service you can offer at every visit.

Summary

  • One fixed reduction gear of about 10:1; reverse by spinning the motor backwards.
  • Regenerative braking turns motion back into battery energy.
  • Regeneration is limited when the battery is full, cold or hot, at very low speed, and in emergency stops.
  • Friction brakes last long but seize from lack of use: clean and lubricate yearly; change fluid every 2 years.
  • EVs are heavy: correct tyres, correct lifting points, never jack under the battery.
