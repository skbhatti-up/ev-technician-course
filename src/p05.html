Hybrid systems in detail

Toyota, Honda, Hyundai/Kia and the Chinese plug-in systems: same goal, different engineering.

You will learn

  • How a hybrid saves fuel
  • How the Toyota power-split system works
  • How Honda, Hyundai/Kia and Chinese DHT plug-in hybrids differ
  • Typical hybrid faults and service points

13.1 How a hybrid saves fuel

  1. Engine off when not needed — at traffic lights, in slow traffic, when coasting.
  2. Electric drive at low speed, where a petrol engine is least efficient.
  3. Regenerative braking — recovering energy that brakes would waste.
  4. Engine kept at its most efficient load. If the engine makes more power than needed, the extra charges the battery; if it makes less, the motor adds the rest.
  5. Efficient engine design. Hybrid engines usually use the Atkinson cycle (late-closing intake valves): less power but better economy. The motor covers the missing power.

These gains are biggest in stop-start city traffic — exactly the conditions of Karachi, Lahore and Rawalpindi.

13.2 Toyota Hybrid System (power split)

Used in the Prius, Aqua, Yaris, Corolla Cross, Camry and Lexus hybrids. There is no clutch, no stepped gearbox, no starter motor and no alternator. One planetary gear set joins three things:

Driving situationWhat happens
Pulling away gentlyEngine off. Battery → MG2 → wheels.
Normal accelerationMG1 spins the engine to start it. Engine power splits: part to wheels, part through MG1 → MG2.
Hard accelerationEngine plus battery power through MG2.
CruisingMostly engine; surplus charges the battery.
BrakingMG2 generates and charges the battery. Engine off.

Battery: NiMH or lithium-ion, about 1–1.5 kWh. Aqua: 144 V (120 cells × 1.2 V). Prius (third generation): 201.6 V (168 cells). A boost converter raises this to as much as 650 V for the motors. The battery is air-cooled by a fan drawing cabin air.

13.3 Honda e:HEV (two-motor system)

Used in the HR-V e:HEV and other recent Honda hybrids. It has a generator motor, a drive motor, and a single clutch.

  • City driving — series mode. The engine turns the generator; the drive motor turns the wheels. Engine speed is not tied to road speed.
  • Steady cruising — engine drive. A clutch connects the engine straight to the wheels through one fixed gear, like top gear.
  • Low load — EV mode. Battery → drive motor.

Workshop tip — do not confuse the two Honda systems

Older imported Honda Vezel and Fit hybrids (2013–2020) use a different system called i-DCD: one motor inside a 7-speed dual-clutch gearbox. These are known for clutch judder and gearbox actuator faults. The newer e:HEV has no such gearbox. Always identify which system you have before diagnosing.

13.4 Hyundai / Kia parallel hybrid

Used in the Tucson, Santa Fe and Sportage hybrids. The layout looks familiar to any mechanic:

Turbo petrol engine → engine clutch → electric motor → 6-speed automatic gearbox → wheels

  • The motor (about 44 kW) sits where the torque converter would be.
  • With the clutch open, the motor alone drives through the gearbox (EV mode). With it closed, engine and motor work together.
  • A second small motor, the HSG (hybrid starter-generator), is belt-driven on the engine to start it smoothly and to generate.
  • Battery: lithium-ion polymer, about 1.5 kWh, 270 V.

13.5 Chinese plug-in hybrids (DHT systems)

BYD (DM-i), GWM Haval (Hi4 / DHT), and Chery / Jaecoo (CSH / SHS) all use a dedicated hybrid transmission (DHT): a compact unit containing a generator, a drive motor, a clutch, and one to three gears for the engine. They behave mainly like an electric car, with the engine helping when needed.

13.6 What is extra on a plug-in hybrid

  • A much bigger battery, on-board charger and charge port — everything from the EV modules applies.
  • Driver modes: EV (electric first), HEV (automatic), and often "save" or "charge" (use the engine to hold or raise the battery level).
  • Engine that rarely runs. If the owner drives mostly on electricity, petrol can sit in the tank for months and go stale; oil can collect moisture because the engine never fully warms up. Many PHEVs force the engine to run occasionally for this reason. Oil changes are still needed by time, even if the engine hours are low.
  • Sealed, pressurised fuel tank on many PHEVs: the fuel flap must be released by a button and may take a few seconds.

13.7 Hybrid faults and service

A hybrid needs all the service of a petrol car (oil, filters, plugs, coolant) plus care of the high-voltage system.

In Pakistan — hybrid battery "repair"

Many shops replace one or two weak modules in an old Aqua or Prius battery. It is cheap and gets the car moving. But the remaining modules are just as old, and new modules that do not match the old ones go out of balance quickly, so the fault often returns within months. A good technician tests all modules, matches replacements by capacity, cleans the bus bars and fan, and is honest with the customer about how long the repair may last compared with a new or fully reconditioned pack.

Danger

A hybrid in READY mode is silent but on. The engine can start at any moment without warning. Before working in the engine bay, switch fully off and keep the key away. And remember: a "small" hybrid battery at 144–270 V is every bit as deadly as a big EV pack.

Summary

  • Hybrids save fuel by stopping the engine, driving electrically at low speed, regenerating, and running the engine efficiently.
  • Toyota: planetary power split, two motor-generators, e-CVT.
  • Honda e:HEV: mostly series, with a direct-drive clutch for cruising. Older Vezel/Fit i-DCD is different.
  • Hyundai/Kia: parallel, motor between engine and 6-speed automatic.
  • Chinese PHEVs: DHT with EV, series and parallel modes, 18–30 kWh batteries.
  • Hybrids need petrol-car servicing plus HV care. Check the 12 V battery first.

14.3 How the car and charger talk

Picture it — knocking on the door

A polite visitor knocks, waits to hear "who is it?", answers, and only walks in when invited. A charger does the same. It "knocks" with a small signal, the car answers, they agree how much current is allowed, the plug locks — and only then does the power come on. Pull the plug and the conversation ends instantly, so the pins are never live in your hand.

No power flows the moment you plug in. First there is a handshake on the control pilot (CP) wire:

  1. Charger puts +12 V on CP. Not connected.
  2. Plug in: a resistor in the car pulls CP down to 9 V. Charger knows a car is present and starts a 1 kHz signal whose pulse width says "you may take up to X amps".
  3. Car is ready: it pulls CP down to 6 V. The plug is locked.
  4. Only now does the charger close its relay and switch on the power.
  5. If anything is wrong — earth missing, plug pulled, fault — CP changes and the power is cut in milliseconds.

For DC charging the car's BMS and the station exchange digital messages continuously: battery voltage, maximum current, temperature. The BMS is in charge; the station supplies exactly the voltage and current the BMS requests.

Measuring the charging signals

When a car will not charge, measuring these low-voltage signals at the inlet tells you whether car and charger are "talking". They are safe, low-voltage tests.

SignalWhat to expect
CP to PE (AC charging, Type 2 and GB/T)+12 V with nothing connected → 9 V when plugged in → 6 V when the car is ready. During charging it is a 1 kHz square wave; the wider the pulse, the more current is allowed.
PP (Type 2) or CC (GB/T AC) to PEA fixed resistor in the plug that tells the car the cable's rating. Roughly: 1.5 kΩ = light-duty cable, 680 Ω = 16–20 A, 220 Ω = 32 A, 100 Ω = 63 A.
CC1 (GB/T DC)The station watches this point: about 12 V unplugged, 6 V while the latch button is pressed, 4 V when fully connected.
S+ / S− (GB/T DC)A CAN bus: about 60 Ω between them with power off; messages visible with a CAN tool.

14.4 The charging curve

Like filling a glass of water, you pour fast at first and slow down near the top. Charging from 80% to 100% can take as long as 10% to 80%. Heat and cold also slow charging: a battery at 45 °C or 5 °C will be limited by the BMS.

Worked example — charging time

Time (h) ≈ Energy needed (kWh) ÷ Charger power (kW), plus about 10% for losses.

A 60 kWh battery from 20% to 80% needs 60% × 60 = 36 kWh.
On a 7 kW wallbox: 36 ÷ 7 × 1.1 ≈ 5.7 hours.
On a 60 kW DC charger: 36 ÷ 60 × 1.1 ≈ 40 minutes.

Danger — earthing

Many Pakistani houses have a poor earth or none at all. A proper EV charger checks the earth and refuses to charge without it. Never bypass this check, and never fit a "no-earth" cheat adapter. Without earth, a fault can make the whole car body live. Fix the earth instead.

  • 3-pin sockets: a portable charger draws 8–13 A for hours. Worn sockets and thin extension leads overheat and burn. Use a good 16 A socket on its own circuit; never a multi-plug extension.
  • Voltage swings and load-shedding: chargers pause when voltage is too low or too high and restart when power returns. Frequent trips point to supply problems, not always the car.
  • Solar: charging in daylight from rooftop solar is the cheapest energy available. Some wallboxes can follow the solar output.
  • UPS and small generators: a domestic UPS cannot charge a car. Generators often have poor earthing and unstable voltage; most chargers will reject them.

15.1 Why this module matters so much

In a petrol car the A/C is a comfort item. In an EV it is part of the drive system, because the same A/C circuit usually cools the battery. That is why A/C work is among the most frequent jobs on electric cars:

  • It runs far more hours: while driving, while parked with the cabin pre-cooled, and during fast charging.
  • The compressor contains high-voltage electronics that are damaged by moisture and wrong oil.
  • There are more valves, sensors and pipes than in a petrol car, so more places to leak or stick.
  • When it is weak, the customer notices twice: a warm cabin and slow charging or reduced power.
What must be kept at temperatureIdeal rangeIf too hot
Battery15–35 °CFaster ageing, reduced power, slow charging, safety risk
Motor and power electronicsCoolant below about 65 °CPower reduced; electronics fail
CabinComfortable for peopleUnhappy customer, and the A/C uses range

15.2 The loops

  • Loop 1 cools the motor, inverter, charger and DC-DC converter through a radiator at the front.
  • Loop 2 circulates coolant through the battery's cold plate. In mild weather it may use a radiator; in hot weather it goes through the chiller.
  • Loop 3 is the air-conditioning. A second branch feeds the chiller (or, on direct-cooled packs such as BYD, plates inside the battery).

Each coolant loop has its own electric pump. There is no engine-driven water pump and often no thermostat; instead there are electric multi-way valves.

15.3 How A/C makes cold: the refrigeration cycle

Picture it — sweat

When sweat dries on your skin, you feel cool. The liquid takes heat from your body to turn into vapour. An A/C system does exactly this, on purpose and in a closed loop: a liquid evaporates inside the cabin unit and takes heat from the air, then is squeezed and condensed back to liquid outside, giving that heat away. A/C does not "make cold". It carries heat from inside to outside.

Table 15.1 — The four stages
StageWhat happensRefrigerant leaves as
1 · CompressionThe compressor sucks in cool gas and squeezes it. Squeezing a gas makes it hot (feel a bicycle pump).Hot, high-pressure gas
2 · CondensationIn the condenser, outside air takes the heat away. The gas turns to liquid.Warm, high-pressure liquid
3 · ExpansionThe liquid is forced through a tiny opening. Pressure falls suddenly and it becomes very cold.Cold, low-pressure mist
4 · EvaporationIn the evaporator, the cold mist boils to gas by taking heat from the cabin air. The air leaves cold and dry.Cool, low-pressure gas

Key idea — remember "C-C-E-E"

Compress → Condense → Expand → Evaporate. Hot and high pressure from the compressor to the valve; cold and low pressure from the valve back to the compressor. If you can draw Figure 15.2 from memory, you can diagnose most A/C faults.

15.5 The electric compressor

Inside one sealed aluminium housing are three things:

  1. The scroll pump — two spiral plates, one fixed and one moving in a small circle. Gas is trapped between the spirals and squeezed towards the centre. Smooth, quiet, few moving parts.
  2. A three-phase motor — a small version of the drive motor, cooled by the refrigerant flowing over it.
  3. An inverter and control board — a small version of the drive inverter, bolted to the housing.

It has two electrical connections: an orange high-voltage connector (300–400 V DC, or more), and a low-voltage connector carrying 12 V, earth, and a LIN or CAN data line. The A/C controller does not switch the compressor on and off like a clutch; it sends a speed command (for example 800 to 8,000 rpm) and the compressor reports back its speed, current and any fault.

What the compressor needs before it will run

  • A cooling request (cabin A/C on, or the battery asking for cooling)
  • High voltage present: car in READY or charging, pre-charge complete, HV fuse good
  • HVIL loop closed and no insulation fault
  • 12 V supply, earth and LIN/CAN communication good
  • Refrigerant pressure inside limits (not empty, not too high)
  • Evaporator not near freezing; battery state of charge not too low

If any one is missing, the compressor stays off. So "compressor not running" is usually a missing condition, not a dead compressor.

Danger — compressor oil

In an electric compressor, the oil touches the motor windings. It must be a special insulating oil (POE type, such as ND-11 — check the label under the bonnet). Normal PAG oil from petrol-car systems conducts electricity. Even a small amount of PAG oil — from a shared A/C machine, hose or oil injector — can cause an insulation fault that stops the whole car, and can make the compressor casing live.

Use dedicated or properly flushed A/C equipment for EVs and hybrids, only the specified oil and refrigerant, and only leak-detection dye that is approved for electric compressors.

15.7 Refrigerant service: recover, evacuate, charge

RefrigerantNotes
R134aCommon on existing cars. Not flammable. A strong greenhouse gas.
R1234yfNewer cars. Mildly flammable. Different service ports and its own service machine.

The type and the exact charge weight in grams are printed on a label under the bonnet. Never mix types. Never release refrigerant to the air: recover it with a machine.

  1. Recover. The machine draws out the old refrigerant and measures how much came out. Compare with the label: a low amount confirms a leak.
  2. Repair the fault. Fit new O-rings with the correct oil. Renew the drier or desiccant if the system has been open for long or the compressor failed.
  3. Evacuate. Pull a deep vacuum for at least 30 minutes. This boils away moisture and removes air.
  4. Vacuum hold test. Close the valves and watch for 10–15 minutes. If the vacuum is lost, there is still a leak. Do not charge.
  5. Charge by weight. Put in exactly the amount on the label. EV systems are very sensitive: too little and too much both cause faults.
  6. Verify. Run the A/C: check pressures, vent temperature, compressor current and fault codes.

Finding leaks

  • Look first: oily, dusty patches at pipe joints, the condenser and service ports.
  • Electronic leak detector: a "sniffer" moved slowly under each joint (refrigerant is heavier than air).
  • Nitrogen pressure test: fill the empty system with dry nitrogen, watch the gauge and use soapy water on joints. Good for larger leaks.
  • UV dye: only a dye approved for electric compressors.

Workshop tip

"Just top it up" is poor practice on any car and worse on an EV. You cannot know how much is in the system without recovering and weighing it, and every top-up through a contaminated machine risks the compressor. Recover, fix the leak, evacuate, charge by weight.

15.8 Heating: heat pump and PTC

There is no hot engine to warm the cabin, so EVs use one or both of these.

  • A PTC heater — an electric heating element running on high voltage (3–6 kW). Simple, but it takes 1 kW of battery energy to make 1 kW of heat.
  • A heat pump — the A/C system running in reverse.

Extra parts in a heat pump system: a reversing valve (or a set of shut-off valves) to change the direction of flow, several electronic expansion valves, an inside condenser for heating, and an accumulator that protects the compressor from sucking in liquid. Typical faults: stuck in one mode, an EXV sticking, valve fault codes, refrigerant charge slightly wrong.

PTC heater checks (system isolated): HV fuse, resistance of the element compared with the manual, insulation resistance to the case, and its control signal. A PTC heater that has let coolant inside is a classic cause of insulation faults.

15.9 Coolant

  • Use only the specified coolant. Battery loops need low-conductivity coolant because it flows close to high-voltage parts.
  • Never top up with tap water. Minerals raise conductivity and cause corrosion and scale.
  • These systems trap air easily. Many need a vacuum filling tool and a scan-tool bleed routine that runs the pumps and opens the valves.
  • A falling coolant level with no visible leak is serious: coolant may be going into the battery pack, motor or chiller.

Replacing a compressor properly:

  1. Isolate the HV system and prove dead. Recover the refrigerant.
  2. Inspect the old oil and the drier. If there are metal particles, the debris has travelled through the system.
  3. Flush the pipes and heat exchangers if contaminated; replace the expansion valve and the drier or desiccant.
  4. Fit the new compressor with the correct type and quantity of oil (new units are often pre-filled; follow the instructions).
  5. New O-rings, correct torque. Evacuate, vacuum hold test, charge by weight.
  6. Check insulation resistance, clear codes, and verify performance.

Skipping the flush and drier is the usual reason a new compressor fails again within weeks.

In Pakistan

Dust, insects and plastic bags block condensers and radiators quickly here. Washing the front heat exchangers gently with water (from the back towards the front, low pressure) and changing the cabin filter are cheap services that directly protect the battery. Many roadside A/C shops share one machine and one oil between all cars; on an EV that can destroy the compressor. In our climate, an EV with a weak A/C is an EV with a battery problem coming.

Summary

  • In an EV the A/C cools the battery as well as the cabin, so A/C faults affect charging and power.
  • Compress → condense → expand → evaporate. High side hot, low side cold.
  • The electric compressor has a scroll pump, motor and inverter; it is speed-controlled over LIN/CAN and needs many conditions before it runs.
  • Only insulating POE oil. PAG oil causes insulation faults.
  • Read both pressures and match the pattern to the cause.
  • Recover, repair, evacuate, hold, charge by weight, verify. Never "top up".
  • A heat pump moves heat and is 2–3 times more efficient than a PTC heater.
  • Use only specified coolant and bleed with the proper procedure.

The 12 V system, networks and software

The low-voltage side: small battery, many computers, and the wires that let them talk.

You will learn

  • Why the 12 V battery is critical in an EV
  • What the main control units do
  • How CAN bus works and how to check it
  • Why software and calibration are part of modern repair

16.1 The 12 V battery

Every EV and hybrid still has a 12 V battery. It powers the computers, lights, locks, wipers and — most importantly — the contactors. If the 12 V battery is flat, the computers cannot wake up and the contactors cannot close. The car is dead, even with a full high-voltage battery.

Key idea

The most common reason an EV or hybrid "will not start" is a weak 12 V battery. Check it first, every time. It is the cheapest and easiest fix in the whole car.

  • The 12 V battery in an EV is small because it never has to crank an engine. It is usually AGM lead-acid; some cars (BYD) use a small 12 V lithium battery.
  • It is charged by the DC-DC converter only when the car is in READY or charging.
  • Typical life is 3–5 years; less in heat.
  • Jump-starting: you can normally boost an EV's 12 V battery from another car or a jump pack to wake it up. Do not use an EV to jump-start a petrol car unless the handbook allows it; the starter current can damage the DC-DC converter.

In Pakistan

Aftermarket trackers, alarms, dash cameras and LED accessories are fitted to almost every car here. Wired badly, they drain the small 12 V battery while the car is parked, or disturb the data network. When you see a repeated flat 12 V battery or strange electrical faults, look for added wiring first.

Finding a parked drain

Connect an ammeter in series with the 12 V negative lead (or use a DC clamp meter). Lock the car and wait 20–30 minutes for the computers to go to sleep. A healthy car draws less than about 30–50 mA. If it is higher, pull fuses one at a time until the current drops to find the circuit responsible.

16.3 CAN bus

Instead of a separate wire for every signal, control units share a pair of wires called a CAN bus and send each other short digital messages, thousands per second.

Picture it — the group chat

A CAN bus works like a group chat. Every member sees every message. Each message starts with a label saying what it is about ("battery temperature", "wheel speed"), and each control unit simply ignores the ones it does not need. If one member goes silent, the others notice and complain: that is a "lost communication" fault code. If someone shouts nonsense continuously (a short circuit on the wires), nobody can talk at all.

Basic CAN checks with a multimeter

TestGood resultMeaning if wrong
Resistance between CAN-H and CAN-L, 12 V battery disconnectedAbout 60 Ω120 Ω → one terminating resistor or part of the bus is open. Near 0 Ω → wires shorted together.
Voltage CAN-H to earth, ignition onAbout 2.5–3.5 V0 V or 12 V → short to earth or to supply
Voltage CAN-L to earth, ignition onAbout 1.5–2.5 VAs above

At the OBD socket (16 pins, under the dashboard): pin 16 is battery positive, pins 4 and 5 are earth, pins 6 and 14 are CAN-High and CAN-Low. This is where the scan tool connects.

A fault code beginning with U (for example U0100, "lost communication with…") means a control unit is not being heard on the network. Check its power supply, earth and connectors before condemning the unit itself.

16.4 Software, coding and calibration

  • Software updates. Makers fix faults and add features by updating software, at the dealer or "over the air" (OTA). Some customer complaints are cured only by an update.
  • Coding and pairing. Many replacement parts (BMS, inverter, charger, even 12 V batteries on some cars) must be registered to the car with a scan tool before they will work.
  • Calibration. After replacing or disturbing certain parts you must run a learning routine: resolver angle offset after motor work, steering angle sensor after alignment, brake pedal sensor, and so on.
  • Driver assistance (ADAS). Front cameras and radars for automatic braking and lane keeping need re-calibration after windscreen replacement, bumper repair, suspension or alignment work.

Workshop tip

Learn to read wiring diagrams. They are the map for every electrical fault. Start by tracing simple circuits (a horn, a brake light) until the symbols for fuses, relays, earths, connectors and splices are familiar. This one skill separates a diagnostician from a parts-changer.

16.5 Reading the instrument panel

The dashboard is the car's first report. Always ask the customer which lights and messages appeared, or look for yourself, before connecting the scan tool. Symbols differ a little between makers, but the colours follow one rule everywhere.

  • READY replaces engine noise. A car showing READY will move if the accelerator is touched.
  • The power gauge replaces the rev counter: it swings one way for power used and the other way for regeneration.
  • Many cars show SOC in %, estimated range, and average consumption in kWh per 100 km. Note these on the job card.
  • A red light together with a text message ("Stop safely", "Check EV system") is the car's own summary of the fault. Write it down word for word.

Summary

  • The 12 V battery wakes the car and closes the contactors. Check it first.
  • Added accessories often cause drains and network faults.
  • Control units share data on CAN bus: two wires, about 60 Ω between them.
  • "U" codes are communication faults: check power, earth and wiring first.
  • Modern repair includes software updates, coding and calibration.
