Part C · The battery

Battery cells and chemistry

The battery is the most expensive part of the car. It starts with a single cell.

You will learn

  • How a lithium-ion cell stores and releases energy
  • The main chemistries: LFP, NMC, NiMH and lead-acid
  • The shapes cells come in, including BYD's Blade
  • Why chemistry matters for safety, life and range

6.1 Inside a lithium-ion cell

A cell has four main parts:

  • Negative electrode (anode) — graphite coated on copper foil.
  • Positive electrode (cathode) — a lithium metal compound coated on aluminium foil. The compound gives the chemistry its name (LFP, NMC…).
  • Separator — a very thin plastic sheet with tiny holes. It keeps the electrodes apart but lets lithium ions pass.
  • Electrolyte — a liquid that carries the ions. It is flammable.

When you charge, the charger pushes lithium ions from the cathode into the graphite anode. When you discharge, they flow back and the electrons flow round the outside circuit, driving the motor. Nothing is burned or used up — ions simply move back and forth. That is why the cell can be recharged thousands of times.

Danger

If the separator is damaged — by a crash, a nail, a manufacturing fault or overheating — the two electrodes touch. This is an internal short circuit. The cell heats very quickly and can catch fire. Never drop, crush, puncture or open a lithium cell.

What this means in practice

  • LFP is tough, safe and long-lived, and it tolerates being charged to 100% often. It stores less energy per kilogram, so the pack is heavier for the same range. Its voltage hardly changes between 20% and 90% charge, which makes it hard for the car to know the exact charge level; makers ask owners to charge to 100% regularly so the BMS can re-calibrate.
  • NMC stores more energy in less weight, so it gives longer range and stronger power. It is less tolerant of heat and of sitting at 100% charge. Makers usually recommend charging to 80% for daily use. Chinese documents call it NCM or "ternary lithium" (三元锂); it is the same chemistry.
  • NiMH is old but reliable. It is heavy and holds little energy, which is fine for a hybrid that only needs 1–2 kWh.
  • Lead-acid is cheap and heavy and wears out quickly when deeply discharged.

In Pakistan

Our summers are hard on batteries. LFP copes with heat better than NMC, which is one reason it suits this market. But no lithium battery likes being parked in direct sun at 45 °C. Advising customers to park in shade is real, useful battery care.

Coming next: sodium-ion cells (cheaper, no lithium, lower energy) are starting to appear in small Chinese EVs and scooters. Solid-state cells, with no liquid electrolyte, are in development. The principles in this course apply to both.

Summary

  • A lithium-ion cell has an anode, cathode, separator and electrolyte. Ions move inside; electrons do work outside.
  • LFP: 3.2 V, safer, longer life, heavier. NMC: 3.7 V, more energy, needs more care.

  • A damaged separator means an internal short and possible fire.

Battery pack design and specifications

How hundreds of cells become one pack, and how to read the numbers that describe it.

You will learn

  • How cells are grouped into modules and packs
  • How to calculate pack voltage and energy
  • The meaning of SOC, SOH, C-rate and usable capacity
  • How packs are cooled, and the difference between 400 V and 800 V systems

7.1 Cell → module → pack

Newer designs remove steps to save weight and space:

  • Cell-to-pack (CTP) — no modules. Cells go straight into the pack. BYD Blade and many CATL packs do this.
  • Cell-to-body (CTB) — the top of the pack is the floor of the car (BYD Seal).

Workshop tip

Older module-type packs can be repaired by swapping one bad module. CTP and CTB packs are much harder to repair at cell level; often the whole pack is exchanged. Knowing which type you have tells you what repair is realistic before you quote a price.

7.2 Pack voltage and energy

Pack layout is written as xS yP: x groups in series, each group having y cells in parallel.

Pack voltage = cells in series × cell voltage
Pack capacity (Ah) = cells in parallel × cell Ah
Pack energy (kWh) = pack voltage × pack Ah ÷ 1,000

Worked example — BYD Atto 3 (extended range)

Published data: 126 Blade cells in series (126S1P), each 3.2 V and 150 Ah.

Voltage = 126 × 3.2 = 403.2 V
Energy = 403.2 × 150 ÷ 1,000 = 60.48 kWh — exactly the figure on the brochure.

Worked example — a typical NMC pack

96S2P using 3.65 V, 78 Ah cells.

Nominal voltage = 96 × 3.65 = 350 V. Capacity = 2 × 78 = 156 Ah. Energy = 350 × 156 ÷ 1,000 = 54.6 kWh.

Fully charged: 96 × 4.2 = 403 V. Empty: 96 × 3.0 = 288 V.

Key idea

Pack voltage is not fixed. It rises as the pack charges and falls as it empties. A "400 V" car may measure anything from about 290 V to 420 V. Always think in terms of minimum, nominal and maximum voltage.

Worked example — C-rate

A 50 kWh pack is charged at a 100 kW fast charger. C-rate = 100 ÷ 50 = 2C. In theory 0–100% in half an hour (in practice the car slows down above 80%).

The same pack on a 7 kW home charger: 7 ÷ 50 = 0.14C — very gentle, about 7 hours.

7.4 Battery power: why a small pack limits performance

A battery can only supply a certain maximum current safely. Since P = V × I, the battery has a maximum power, and the motor cannot use more than the battery can give.

  • A bigger pack (more cells in parallel) can give more current, so more power.
  • A cold, hot, old or nearly empty pack can give less. The BMS tells the car to reduce power — the driver feels this as weak acceleration or sees a "tortoise" warning light.
  • Under heavy current, voltage sags: Vdrop = I × Rinternal. An aged pack with high internal resistance sags more and heats more.

This is why a hybrid with a 1.5 kWh battery can only drive electrically at low power, while a 60 kWh BEV pack can deliver 150 kW easily. Module 11 returns to this.

7.5 Keeping the pack at the right temperature

Lithium cells are happiest between about 15 °C and 35 °C. Too hot and they age quickly or become unsafe. Too cold and they give less power and cannot be charged quickly.

In Pakistan

With 45 °C summers, battery cooling works very hard. A weak air-conditioning system is not only a comfort problem — on many EVs it will slow charging, cut power and shorten battery life. Dust is our other enemy: blocked battery fan filters on used Aqua and Prius cars are one of the most common causes of early hybrid battery failure here.

7.6 400 V and 800 V systems

Most EVs today are called "400 V" (roughly 300–450 V). Newer high-end platforms use "800 V" (roughly 550–900 V).

  • Advantage of 800 V: half the current for the same power, so thinner cables, less heat, and much faster DC charging (250 kW and above).
  • Cost: more cells in series, more expensive power electronics (silicon carbide), and stricter insulation.
  • For you: the safety procedure is the same, but your meter and gloves must be rated for the voltage. A CAT III 1,000 V meter and Class 0 gloves cover both.

7.7 The pack case

The pack is a sealed metal case, usually rated IP67 (dust-tight and able to survive short immersion in water). On it you will find the HV connectors, low-voltage signal connector, coolant pipes, a pressure-relief vent, the service disconnect on many models, and a data label.

  • Cells → modules → pack, or cells straight to pack (CTP).

  • Cooling is by air, liquid or refrigerant. Heat and dust are the big enemies in Pakistan.

Battery management and protection

The BMS, contactors and pre-charge circuit: how the pack looks after itself.

You will learn

  • What the battery management system (BMS) measures and controls
  • How contactors and the pre-charge circuit connect the battery to the car
  • What cell balancing is and why one weak cell limits the whole pack
  • What thermal runaway is, and what shortens battery life

8.1 What the BMS does

A lithium cell is damaged by over-charging, over-discharging, too much current and extreme temperature. The BMS exists to stop these things happening.

JobHow
MeasureVoltage of every cell (or cell group), pack voltage, pack current, and temperatures at many points
CalculateSOC, SOH, and how much power the pack can safely give or accept right now
ProtectOpens the contactors if voltage, current or temperature go outside safe limits
BalanceKeeps all cells at the same charge level
Control temperatureRequests cooling or heating
Monitor insulationChecks resistance between the HV system and the car body
CommunicateTalks to the vehicle computer and the charger over CAN bus; stores fault codes

The BMS usually has a master controller and several slave boards, one on each module or group of cells, which read the cell voltages and temperatures.

8.2 Inside the pack: contactors and pre-charge

Contactors are heavy-duty relays. They are opened and closed by 12 V signals from the BMS. When the car is off, all contactors are open and no voltage leaves the pack.

Why pre-charge is needed

The inverter contains a large capacitor. An empty capacitor behaves like a short circuit for an instant. If the main contactors closed straight onto it, a huge current would rush in and weld the contacts together.

Picture it — the canal gate

Open a big gate suddenly onto a dry canal and the first rush of water tears at the banks. A careful operator opens a small side gate first, lets the canal fill gently, and only then lifts the main gate. Nothing rushes, because the levels are already equal. The pre-charge resistor is the small side gate; the inverter capacitor is the dry canal.

So the car connects in stages:

  1. Main negative contactor closes. Click.
  2. Pre-charge contactor closes. Current flows gently through the resistor and fills the capacitor. This takes a fraction of a second.
  3. BMS checks the voltage on the inverter side. When it reaches about 95% of pack voltage, pre-charge is complete.
  4. Main positive contactor closes. Click. The pre-charge contactor opens. The car shows READY.

Workshop tip

Listen when a car is switched to READY: you can hear the contactors click in sequence. If the voltage does not rise during pre-charge (for example, because of a short circuit or a failed resistor), the BMS stops and stores a "pre-charge failure" fault code. If a contactor has welded shut, the BMS will detect voltage where there should be none and refuse to start. These are common, logical faults you will learn to diagnose.

8.3 Cell balancing: the weakest cell rule

Cells in series are like links in a chain. All carry the same current. But no two cells are perfectly identical; over time some hold slightly more charge than others.

Balancing corrects this. In passive balancing (the common type), the BMS switches a small resistor across the highest cells to bleed off a little charge, usually near the end of charging. In active balancing, charge is moved from high cells to low cells.

Key idea

On a scan tool, look at the difference between the highest and lowest cell voltage. A healthy pack at rest shows a spread of a few millivolts (for example under 0.02–0.03 V). A large spread, or one cell that drops quickly under load, points to a failing cell or module. This single reading is one of the most useful in battery diagnosis.

8.4 Insulation monitoring

Remember that the HV system is insulated from the car body. The BMS (or a separate unit) keeps measuring the resistance between HV+ and the body, and HV− and the body. Healthy values are in megohms. Regulations require at least 100 Ω per volt for DC parts and 500 Ω per volt where AC is present — for a 400 V car that is 40 kΩ to 200 kΩ as an absolute minimum.

If the resistance falls — because of water, damaged cable insulation, coolant leaking into the pack, or a failed A/C compressor — the car logs an "insulation fault" (also called isolation fault or leakage fault) and may refuse to drive or charge. Finding these is a core EV diagnostic skill (Module 18).

8.5 Thermal runaway

Thermal runaway is when a cell heats itself faster than it can cool. Heat triggers chemical reactions that make more heat. The cell vents hot, toxic, flammable gas and may burn, and the heat can spread to the next cell.

Causes

  • Crash or impact damage from below
  • Internal short from a manufacturing defect
  • Over-charging (failed BMS or wrong charger)
  • External short circuit
  • Outside fire or extreme heat
  • Water inside the pack

Warning signs

  • Sweet, solvent-like or burning smell
  • Hissing or popping from the pack
  • White or grey "smoke" (vent gas)
  • Pack case hot to the back of the hand
  • Swollen case or modules
  • Battery temperature warning on dash

Danger

If you see any of these signs: stop work, move people away, move the car outside if it is safe to do so, and call the fire service. Do not try to open the pack. LFP is much harder to push into runaway than NMC, but no chemistry is immune.

8.6 What makes a battery age

All batteries slowly lose capacity. A well-treated EV battery typically keeps more than 80% after 8–10 years. These things speed up ageing:

Bad for the batteryAdvice you can give customers
High temperaturePark in shade or a garage. Keep the cooling system serviced.
Sitting at 100% for long periods (especially NMC)For daily use charge NMC to 80%. Charge to 100% just before a long trip. Follow the maker's advice for LFP.
Sitting near 0%Do not leave the car parked empty. Store at 40–60% if leaving it for weeks.
Frequent DC fast chargingUse home AC charging for normal days; fast chargers for journeys.
Poor-quality or wrong chargersEspecially on e-bikes: use the correct charger for the battery.

Opening a pack is Level 4 work (Module 21) and is taught only under supervision. But you should understand the measurements, because they explain every battery report you will read.

Why cells must be matched

A replacement module or cell must match the others in capacity, internal resistance and state of charge. A new, strong cell among old, tired ones does not rescue the pack: the pack is still limited by the weakest cell, and the mismatch makes balancing harder. This is "grading and matching", and it is why a proper repair measures every cell and does not simply swap the one that failed.

Summary

  • The BMS measures every cell, calculates SOC and SOH, protects, balances and communicates.
  • Contactors connect the pack to the car. Pre-charge fills the inverter capacitor gently first.
  • A pack is limited by its weakest cell. Check the cell voltage spread.
  • Insulation monitoring watches for leakage between HV and the body.
  • Thermal runaway is rare but serious. Know the warning signs.
  • Heat, extreme SOC and constant fast charging shorten battery life.
